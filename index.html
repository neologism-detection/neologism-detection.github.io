Multimodal AI Detection In Two Words

Daniel Fein1, Arpita Singhal1, Maneesh Agrawala1, and Maty Bohacek1

1 Stanford University

AI-generated text, images, audio, and video are now ubiquitous and hard for people to identify. Meanwhile, detectors built to catch AI-generated content are fragmented along modality lines and can only explain their predictions by imitating human-written rationales. We address both of these issues by extending neologism learning from language-model steering to multimodal classification: we add two tokens, <REAL> and <AIGEN>, to the vocabulary of an otherwise-frozen multimodal LLM and train only their two embedding rows so that the model's next-token distribution, under a paired prompt, encodes the real-vs-AI decision. Because every modality is projected into the same embedding space, one token pair can cover text, image, audio, and video simultaneously, without per-modality heads, and the same frozen backbone can be asked, in plain English, what <AIGEN> has come to mean.

Contemporary detectors of AI-generated images and AI-generated speech generally have very little in common, as their architectures and training mixtures differ completely. The one commonality is perhaps that the way such models generate explanations for their predictions is based on mimicking human-produced descriptions — if the models have any explanation capability, that is. Troublingly, this comes at a time when humans are already virtually unable to distinguish between AI-generated and human-produced content. Both of these problems come from the same place: the decision is often stored in a classifier head that has no vocabulary and no relationship to anything else the model knows. To solve them, we realized the decision had to be moved. Conveniently, multimodal LLMs already project text, pixels, audio, and video frames into one embedding space, offering a unified direction space for all of these modalities — and because this direction lives among the model's own word embeddings, the model can later be asked to explain itself in its own words.

Key contributions
  • One token pair for four modalities. We extend neologism learning from steering to classification: two embedding rows in an otherwise-frozen multimodal LLM detect AI-generated text, image, audio and video, with no per-modality head anywhere in the system.
  • Competitive detection from a 12 KB checkpoint. The pair matches or beats purpose-built detectors that train millions of parameters, and leads the strongest dedicated video baseline by 0.27 AUROC out of distribution.
  • A detector that explains itself. Because the two rows are ordinary vocabulary entries, the frozen model can be asked what it learned — and that description alone lifts an off-the-shelf frontier model from chance to 0.72–0.75 pairwise accuracy.

Method

Neologism learning was introduced as a way to steer a language model: append a single token that the tokenizer has never before seen, give it a fresh row in the embedding matrix, freeze everything else, and train that one row until the token means what is desired. Instead of steering, we use this method for classification. <REAL> and <AIGEN>, our two new tokens, are dropped into the same prompt template alongside the input in question — “<TOK> <image> This is a <TOK>” — and the template is run twice, once with each token. The score is the difference between the log-probabilities the model assigns to one fixed continuation word under the two prompts. As a result, the whole trained artifact is two vectors.

Method overview. (A) Both tokens are paired with the input in contrastive prompts. Only their two rows of the embedding matrix receive gradient. (B) At inference time, the log-probabilities over these two tokens produce a single score, sm(x), which applies to any modality the backbone accepts at input. (C) Because the rows sit among ordinary word embeddings and the output head is tied to them, the same frozen model can be asked what the learned tokens mean.

Effectively, this method can be seen as a linear probe on top of a pre-trained embedding space. What distinguishes it from an ordinary probe is that the direction lives inside a language model's vocabulary, so it can be decoded back into language.

Cross-modal capabilities

The learned token pair is modality-agnostic by construction. It can be used for any modality so long as there is an encoder mapping it into the shared embedding space. This invites an empirical question: is the direction learned for AI-generated versus real content in any way shared across modalities? We test this by withholding each modality, training on the other three, and then evaluating on the fourth, unseen modality.

We find that text is meaningfully recoverable from the other three modalities, reaching an AUROC of 0.73. For image, there appears to be some signal, at an AUROC of 0.60. For audio and video, there appears to be little to no signal learned from the others, as the resulting models are left guessing at 0.48 and 0.47.

Training on all four modalities is, as expected, the most balanced setting: text and audio at 0.99, video at 0.80, and image at 0.68.

Explaining the predictions

Every explainable detector we know of learns its explanations from rationales written by human annotators. As a result, these methods may produce text merely optimized to be plausible, leaving little to no way of verifying faithfulness. Our tokens are optimized only to be discriminative; they are never shown a word of explanation during training. While that does not guarantee faithfulness on its own, it skips the ambiguous optimization step.

Text
<AIGEN> text is characterized by…
Brevity — short sentences and paragraphs, often focused on conveying a single idea. Conciseness — avoidance of unnecessary words or description. Directness — straightforward, unambiguous language with minimal use of metaphor. Objectivity — a neutral or detached tone, often lacking emotional appeals or personal opinion.
Image
<AIGEN> images are characterized by…
Geometric and sharp — clean lines and precise angles; shapes are rarely organic or blurry. Minimalist and sparse — a lot of negative space, few elements competing for attention. Monochromatic or limited palette — a restricted colour scheme, frequently black, white and greys.

Global self-verbalizations. Greedy completions decoded from an otherwise-unchanged multimodal LLM whose only learnable parameters were the two embedding rows. Nothing in training asked the model to produce these; they are read out of the same frozen weights that do the detection.

We test faithfulness in two ways. First, we ask the trained model for words characteristic of <AIGEN> text and words characteristic of <REAL> text, and then push each word back through the detector on its own. The words we get for <AIGEN> do score AI-like, with a 0.91 log-probability gap in the expected direction.

Second, we take the description the model wrote about each of the learned tokens and hand the resulting plain English to an off-the-shelf frontier model, as a system prompt for detection in an LLM-as-a-judge setting. Note that this frontier model has not seen the training data and has never been explicitly trained for this task.
